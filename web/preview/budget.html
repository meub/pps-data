What's driving costs up

Growth since 2022-23, with each line starting at 0%. Benefits have grown much faster than salaries, while the number of students has fallen.

    Can PPS borrow to cover the gap?

    Not for day-to-day operations. Oregon's Local Budget Law requires each year's budget to balance, so PPS has to close a gap before it adopts a budget. Its cushion is its reserve, which is at the 5% minimum ($41 million).

    PPS does borrow for other purposes: voter-approved construction bonds (which can only pay for buildings), pension bonds (issued in 2002, 2003, and 2021, and repaid through payroll costs), and short-term loans to smooth cash flow within a year. Its outstanding bond and other debt is about $2.1 billion (Volume 1, p. 23). PPS's finance office is the authority on exactly what the district may borrow for.

    Cost per student and school size

    Each dot is one school. PPS gives every school a principal, an office assistant, and a support position regardless of size, so those fixed costs are spread over fewer students in a small school. Schools with more students from low-income families also receive extra federal Title I and equity staffing.

    Show as a table

    "Dollars per student" is PPS's figure from the Individual School Reports: the school's General Fund, Title I, and grant dollars divided by its enrollment. It leaves out staff PPS assigns centrally (special education, English language development, nutrition, custodians), so it is lower than the full cost of running a school. Enrollment is PPS's 2026-27 projection, the number it used to staff each school.

    What closing schools might save Estimate, not a PPS figure

    PPS hasn't published how much each scenario would save. This is a rough estimate built from PPS's own budget numbers: the staff and building costs that go away when a school closes. Most teachers and student services move with the students, so they aren't counted as savings.

    How it's estimated (2026-27 dollars, per year):

      Not included:

      • Added costs at receiving schools, such as an assistant principal when a school grows past PPS's staffing thresholds, or longer bus routes.
      • One-time costs: moving, building closure, and transition support.
      • Savings from filling partly empty classrooms at receiving schools, which could be larger than the teacher estimate here.
      • Program moves, boundary changes, and grade changes in the scenarios.
      • State funding follows students, so closures don't change revenue directly.

      Methodology & sources

      Sources

        How the numbers were put together

        • The money-in and money-out figures are the 2026-27 adopted General Fund (Volume 1, pp. 103 and 107-108). Uses are PPS's accounting function codes, grouped and renamed in plain language. Two lines PPS books as negative district-wide adjustments are folded into their groups.
        • "Revenue" in the gap chart is each year's General Fund resources minus the money carried over from the year before. "Spending" leaves out the required reserve. The forecast is PPS's own (Volume 1, p. 113), and the shortfall labels are the amounts PPS prints.
        • Average cost per position is General Fund salaries plus benefits ($680.5 million) divided by budgeted General Fund positions (4,481 FTE). It mixes teachers, custodians, and administrators, so it is a rough figure.
        • School figures come from Volume 2, which is the May 2026 proposed budget. The adopted budget changed some school allocations, so a few schools may differ slightly from their final 2026-27 numbers.
        • Scenario markings use PPS's October 2026 draft rightsizing scenarios, as on the main dashboard.

        What this page doesn't show

        • A PPS estimate of scenario savings. PPS has not published one; the savings section is this site's estimate from budget figures, and building operating costs (utilities, upkeep) are not broken out by school in the budget.
        • Bond-funded construction, which voters approve separately and can't be spent on teachers or programs.
